Saturday Coffee & Tea · Trading Psychology

Why Pain
Outlasts the Win

Good days are real. So why does one hard day get to live in us longer than ten good ones, and what do we do about it?

UNDERSTAND · PROPORTION · CELEBRATE

Last time we looked at what the mind does. This time we go to the root. Many of us can tell you every win we've had, and still one bad day makes us want to quit. In trading, in family life, in health. The pain stays and the good days slip.

Here's the thread we'll follow: pain isn't always heavier than joy. It's often just replayed more. And if that's true, then what we do after a bad moment matters more than the bad moment. That's good news, because you can train it.

The Root
The Mind Can Heal Itself. So Why Doesn't It?

"For most people, pain fades faster than joy. When it doesn't, something is feeding it."

The Built-In Healer

Researchers who study memory for emotional events describe the fading affect bias: the feeling attached to unpleasant memories tends to fade faster than the feeling attached to pleasant ones. In plain words, the mind is wired to keep more of the good. Most of us start out with a healer on board.

FADES FASTER
Fading affect

In people struggling with low mood, the healer weakens

In the research reviewed, non-dysphoric people showed a robust bias, but dysphoric people did not. For them, unpleasant and pleasant emotions faded at the same rate. In a larger follow-up (N = 337), people with the highest dysphoria levels had negative emotions that faded less and positive emotions that faded more. The authors say the reasons are still untested.

For you: If your good days feel rare and your bad ones feel permanent, that isn't weakness or a flaw in you. It's a known pattern, and it means the gap can narrow.
As reviewed in the Walker & Skowronski fading affect bias article (Skowronski lab, Northern Illinois University). The review does not discuss rumination.

What Feeds the Pain

One of the best-studied feeders has a name: rumination. It means repetitively and passively focusing on your distress, its possible causes and its possible consequences. It isn't problem solving. It's replaying.

REPLAY
Nolen-Hoeksema

Replaying a hard moment keeps it going and doesn't fix it

A major review concluded that rumination exacerbates and prolongs distress. It also impairs problem solving: people who ruminate tend to see their problems as overwhelming, and they can come up with solutions but are less likely to act on them. It can also strain social support, because the people around us get worn out by the same story.

For you: Replaying a losing trade at 2 a.m. feels like you're working on it. The research suggests you're mostly feeding it.
Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky (2008), Perspectives on Psychological Science, 3(5). Mostly depression research. Rumination predicts the start of depression more consistently than how long it lasts.

The Wipe-Out
Why One Bad Day Erases Everything, and Makes You Want to Quit

"There are two different wipe-outs. Only one of them is in your head."

Wipe-Out One: The Account

Sometimes the bad day really did erase the wins, in dollars. That isn't a mindset problem. That's a sizing problem. Slide it and watch.

Twelve winning days shown. A simple illustration of proportion, not a rule.

The consistency threshold: Good days start to outweigh bad ones when your bad days are small enough to survive. That's what a daily loss limit buys. It's our working rule of thumb, not a research finding: cap your worst day so it costs one or two good days, never ten.

Wipe-Out Two: The Feeling

The second wipe-out happens even when the numbers are fine. A stretch of wins gets erased by one hard day, and you still want to quit. Psychology has a close name for it.

LAPSE → QUIT
Marlatt

It isn't the slip. It's the story we tell about the slip

Marlatt's relapse model, from addiction research, describes the abstinence violation effect. After a lapse, people who blame themselves with "my personal failure" or "I have no willpower" feel guilt and negative emotion and are more likely to abandon the whole attempt. People who blame a specific situation they failed to cope with are more likely to learn from it. The authors stress that moving from lapse to relapse is not inevitable.

For you: "I'm a bad trader" makes you want to quit. "I traded without my stop on a gap day" gives you something to fix. Same day. Different story.
Larimer, Palmer & Marlatt (1999), Alcohol Research & Health, 23(2), 151–160. Built on alcohol relapse. Applying it to trading is our analogy.

Verdict or Event?

Our framing: files you label as verdicts never close. Files you label as events can be learned from and shelved.

Verdict"I always blow it after a good run."
Event"I broke my size rule on Thursday after three wins."
Verdict"I'm not cut out for this."
Event"I didn't stop when I hit my limit. That's the one thing to fix."
Verdict"Everything was ruined today."
Event"One hour went badly. The other hours were fine."
IT FADES
Gilbert

We overestimate how long bad feelings will last

In experiments on affective forecasting, people were generally unaware of the mental mechanisms that ease negative feelings, so they tended to overestimate the duration of their emotional reactions to negative events.

For you: "I want to quit" is often a forecast: "this feeling will last forever." The research says that forecast tends to run long. Don't make a permanent decision from a temporary feeling.
Gilbert, Pinel, Wilson, Blumberg & Wheatley (1998), "Immune neglect," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 75(3), 617–638. Lab experiments.
Mind Hack: The 72-hour rule. Make no big decision about quitting inside 72 hours of a bad day. You can feel it fully. You just don't get to sign anything. (Our rule of thumb, not a studied number.)

The No
Daily Life Runs on Trading Rules

"You can't be disciplined at the desk and scattered everywhere else. It's one person making all of those decisions."

Your Trading Rules Already Have a Life Version

Daily loss limit
A limit on what a day is allowed to cost you: energy, money, patience, hours.
Position size
How much of yourself you put into any one thing. No single thing gets everything.
The stop
The point where you leave a conversation, a task or a mood, decided before you're in it.
Sitting out
"Not today" to a request, an invitation or an urge that doesn't fit the plan.
Revenge trade
The snack, the text, the argument, the scroll you reach for after a bad moment to feel something else.

Saying No to Others

  • Short, kind, no essay. "I can't take that on today."
  • Offer a when, if you mean it. "I can do Thursday."
  • Every no is a yes to something. The plan, your rest, your focus. Say the yes to yourself first.

Saying No to Yourself

  • "Not now" with a time. "Not today. I'll look at it Monday at 9." It's easier to accept than "never."
  • If–then, written in advance. "If I feel the urge to revenge trade, then I stand up and leave the room for ten minutes."
  • Make the no easier than the yes. Log out, delete the app, put the phone in another room.
Mind Hack: Treat a "no" like a position you're holding. It has a size, a reason and an exit. You don't have to defend it to anyone, including yourself.

The Walk-Away
When to Walk Away, From Anything

"The hardest walk away is from the thing we keep going back to."

It might be the screen. It might be a habit, a situation, or something small and hard to name. We say we'll leave and then we're back, again and again. We'll keep this general on purpose. Everyone in the room can fill in their own.

Why the Small Things Hold Us

It's rarely the big thing. It's usually something small that keeps the door open. Our read, as a few common hooks:

  • "Just one more." One more trade, one more try, one more chance to make it right.
  • The familiar. Even pain is predictable, and predictable feels safe.
  • What we've already put in. Leaving feels like admitting that the time was wasted.
  • Fear of what ending it means. Sometimes we aren't holding on to the thing. We're holding on to avoid the feeling of letting go.

Three Questions Before You Decide

  1. Is this still serving the plan?Not "has it ever." Is it, today, moving me toward what I said I wanted?
  2. If I weren't already in it, would I choose it today?If the answer is no, you're staying because of the past, not because of the present.
  3. What am I actually holding on to?The thing itself, or the fear of what comes after letting go? Name it out loud.

Three Steps, Smallest First

  • Pause. Step away for ten minutes. Nothing is decided.
  • Step back. A day, or a set distance. You reduce the size, the contact or the time.
  • Leave. A decision made from your plan, not from the loudest moment.

Pause and step back are not weakness. They're how you test whether leaving is right, without needing to be sure first.

22 OF 32
Kahneman

People judge an episode mostly by its worst moment and how it ends

In a lab study, 32 people put a hand in painfully cold water twice: once for 60 seconds, and once for 60 seconds plus 30 more as the water was slowly warmed a little. 22 of 32 chose to repeat the longer trial, the one with more total pain, because it ended a bit better. The authors treat this as a memory mistake.

For you: How you leave matters. If you walk away on purpose, with a clean ending, you remember it as a decision. If you leave in a blaze, you remember the blaze. Choose your ending.
Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier (1993), Psychological Science, 4(6), 401–405. Small lab sample (university students).
A note of care: If staying in a situation affects your safety or your health, that isn't a trading-psychology question. Please reach out to a trusted person or a professional.
Mind Hack: Set your walk-away rule while you're calm. "If ___ happens, I walk." Then when the moment comes, you aren't deciding. You're following your own rule.

The Proportion
A Better Word Than "Balance"

"A little here, a little there, not too much of anything and not too little of anything. And room to breathe."

Four Word Options

Proportion

Our pick. It comes straight from trading: position sizing and rebalancing. You don't ask whether everything is equal. You ask whether each thing is the right size. Rebalancing is a small adjustment, not a collapse.

Margin

The room and grace. Space left unfilled in your day so one surprise doesn't break it.

Steadiness

Less about splitting your time, more about staying level through it.

Enough

The permission word. A good-enough day counts.

Our suggestion is Proportion with Margin: right-sized, with room to spare.

The Celebration
Check It Off and Celebrate the Day

"This is a good day. Check it off. Celebrate it. We don't do enough of that."

Why Small Wins Count

76%
Amabile & Kramer

Small steps forward shape how the whole day feels

Amabile and Kramer analyzed diaries from 238 workers on 26 project teams, close to 12,000 entries. Steps forward showed up on 76% of people's best-mood days. Of events that had a minor impact on the project, 28% had a major impact on how people felt. On the worst days, setbacks showed up on 67%.

For you: You don't need a big win to have a good day. A small, real step counts, and your mood takes notice.
Amabile & Kramer (2011), "The power of small wins," Harvard Business Review. Correlational study of knowledge workers, not traders.

Today's Check-Off

Tap what's true for you. Add your own.

Close the Day on Purpose

  1. Check off what went right.Before you review what went wrong.
  2. Talk to yourself like a coach, by name.In one lab study, using your own name rather than "I" in your head gave people distance, less distress, and better-rated performance under stress. Try "Coach, here's what you did well today."
  3. Do one small thing to celebrate.A cup of tea. A walk. A call. Let the day end well.
3 PARTS
Neff

Grace has a shape: kindness, shared humanity, awareness

Neff describes self-compassion as treating yourself with the kindness and support you'd show a good friend. It has three parts: self-kindness instead of self-judgment, common humanity ("everyone fails, makes mistakes and gets it wrong sometimes"), and mindful awareness of painful thoughts without being swallowed by them.

For you: Giving yourself grace isn't lowering the bar. It's how you stay in the game long enough to clear it.
Neff & Dahm, "Self-compassion: What it is, what it does, and how it relates to mindfulness," describing Neff (2003). Breines & Chen (2012) found self-compassion increased motivation to improve in small student samples.
A note of care: If heaviness follows you around most days, not just after rough ones, please talk to a counselor or therapist. It's a strength. This session is trading psychology, not treatment.
Being straight about the limits: The fading affect finding comes from people reporting on memories. Rumination research is mostly about depression. The lapse-to-quit idea comes from addiction work. The cold-water study is small and done in a lab, and the diary study is correlational. Connecting these to trading is our read. The rules of thumb here (72 hours, one notch, the loss cap) are suggestions to test, not proven numbers.

Pain lasts longest where we keep replaying it. Joy lasts longest where we stop to celebrate it.
