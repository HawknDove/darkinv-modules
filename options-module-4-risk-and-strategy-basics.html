Risk & Strategy Basics

Time Decay, the Greeks (Kid Version), and Position Sizing for Options

KEY CONCEPT

Time Decay — Your Option Is Melting

THE KID VERSION "An option is like an ice cube. The moment you buy it, it starts melting — a little every day, even if nothing else changes. That melting is called time decay. The closer it gets to the expiration date, the faster it melts. That's why holding options too long, hoping and waiting, quietly costs you money even in a sideways market."

THE GREEKS, SIMPLIFIED

The 4 Forces Acting on Every Option

"The Greeks" sound intimidating, but each one is really just answering one plain-English question about your option.

Delta — "How much does my option move?"

If delta is 0.50, the option gains/loses about $0.50 for every $1 the stock moves. Closer to 1.00 = moves almost like the stock itself.

Theta — "How fast is it melting?"

The dollar amount your option loses per day from time decay alone, all else being equal.

Vega — "How much does fear/excitement affect it?"

Sensitivity to implied volatility. When the market gets nervous, option premiums often rise even if the stock hasn't moved yet.

Gamma — "Is delta speeding up?"

How fast delta itself changes. Highest right around the strike price — this is where option prices can move fastest.

RISK MANAGEMENT

Position Sizing for Options

Options are already leveraged, so the DARKINV 1-2% account risk rule matters even more here than in futures — one contract can represent a bigger swing in your account than you expect.

Before Buying Any Option

  • Know the full premium cost × 100 before entering
  • Risk no more than 1-2% of account on the trade
  • Have a defined exit — a target premium gain AND a max loss you'll accept
  • Check the expiration date gives your thesis enough time to play out

Common Beginner Mistakes

  • Buying the cheapest, shortest-dated option "for more contracts"
  • Holding through expiration hoping for a miracle instead of closing for a partial loss
  • Ignoring that a stock can move correctly and the option can still lose money to time decay
  • Sizing a position on excitement instead of a fixed risk percentage
⚠️ It's possible to be right about direction and still lose money on an option — if the move happens too slowly, or too close to expiration. Direction alone isn't the whole trade.
